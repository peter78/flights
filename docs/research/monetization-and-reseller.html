Monetization & Reseller Legality

Four ways to make money selling flights, trains, and attractions worldwide — from zero-overhead affiliate to bonded OTA — with the regulatory cost of each.

The three regulatory layers

Every monetization model has to clear three layers, in rough order of difficulty:

  1. Accreditation / appointment — who's allowed to sell which inventory. Airlines (IATA / ARC) are the strictest; trains vary by operator; attractions are mostly open marketplaces.
  2. Payments & consumer protection — who's liable if a flight cancels or you go bust. EU Package Travel Directive, UK ATOL, US Seller-of-Travel laws, refund rules.
  3. Marketing & pricing — full-fare disclosure (DOT Part 399 in the US, EU 1008/2008), drip-pricing bans, mandatory ancillary disclosures.

The first two are where startups stall. The third is what gets you fined later if you ignore it.

Model 1 — Affiliate / deep-link

You book nothing. The user clicks through your product to a partner's checkout. The partner pays you a commission. In legal terms you're a publisher, not a seller of travel.

Upside

  • Lightest legal posture by an order of magnitude. No accreditation, no bonding, no payment processing, no refund liability.
  • Launch in days. Most partners self-serve onboarding.
  • Globally portable — same model works US / EU / APAC.

Downside

  • Commissions are low and shrinking. Air affiliate is typically 1–4%; rail varies 3–7% (Trainline) / 5–8% (Rail Europe); attractions are best at 6–10%.
  • You don't own the booking flow — the partner's UX changes flow straight through to your conversion.
  • Attribution attrition: users abandon at the partner site; you only earn on completed bookings, with 7–30 day cookie windows.
  • A subscription overlay is awkward — you can't promise discounted bookings you don't control.

Model 2 — Full reseller / OTA

You take payment, you issue tickets, you eat refund liability. Booking.com, Expedia, Kiwi.com (since their pivot) all run this.

Upside

  • End-to-end customer relationship — re-marketing, ancillary upsell, loyalty programs all yours.
  • Higher per-unit margin: markup + IATA commission + ancillary attach (bags, seats, insurance — often higher margin than the ticket itself).
  • You can charge your own service fees on top (Model 4 variant B).

Downside

  • Accreditation is a real project — most startups stall here.
  • Bonding / insurance ties up working capital permanently.
  • Refund liability: when the airline drags on a refund, you're on the hook. Norwegian, Wow Air, Thomas Cook all left OTAs with massive refund holes.
  • Payment processing risk: chargebacks for cancelled flights run 3–8% of GMV. Stripe / Adyen require sizable reserves.

Rail and attraction reselling are dramatically lighter than air. Rail operators (Trainline, SNCF, DB, Eurostar, Amtrak) offer B2B partner / distributor agreements with no public accreditation regime — expect revenue share plus minimum-volume commitments. Attractions marketplaces (Viator, GetYourGuide, Tiqets, Klook) let you sell with no accreditation but require you to handle payment and refunds yourself; some require their checkout (collapsing back to Model 1).

Important details

  • EU Package Travel Directive (2018). The moment you bundle two travel components for the same trip (flight + hotel, flight + train, flight + tour), you become a package organiser with strict liability and mandatory insolvency insurance. Multi-stop trip planning sits right on this line — see Places & POI.
  • DOT Part 399 ("full-fare advertising"). US: taxes and mandatory fees must be in the headline price. Optional ancillaries can be drip-priced but must be disclosed before payment. See Cost Transparency — the existing fee breakdown is already compliant.
  • DOT 24-hour refund rule. US flights sold by anyone must be refundable within 24 hours of booking. No exceptions.
  • Mistake fares / hidden-city / throwaway ticketing. Airline contracts of carriage forbid these. Resellers who facilitate them have been sued — United v. Skiplagged is the leading US precedent.
  • EU PSD2 SCA drives heavy Strong Customer Authentication friction on EU cards — build in Adyen / Stripe Radar from day one.

Model 3 — Subscription discount club

Users pay you a recurring fee for access to curated deals, alerts, and tools. You're not selling tickets — you're selling information. Users book themselves, often via your affiliate links. Going (formerly Scott's Cheap Flights), Dollar Flight Club, Pomelo, Jack's Flight Club, Travelzoo all run this template.

Upside

  • Best margins of any model. Subscription is high-margin SaaS economics applied to travel. Going was acquired by Hopper's parent in 2024 at a reported 9-figure valuation.
  • No accreditation needed in any jurisdiction — you're a publisher selling subscriptions, not a travel seller.
  • Stripe Subscriptions covers payments — no travel-specific PSP needed.
  • Compatible with affiliate stacking — subscribers click your links and you double-dip.

Downside

  • The "discount" is curation + speed, not negotiated rates. You can't offer subscribers a lower price than Google Flights — you find deals they wouldn't have searched for, faster.
  • Content treadmill: a steady drumbeat of genuinely good deals, or churn explodes. Going employs ~25 flight experts full-time.
  • Mistake-fare risk: airlines can (and do) cancel error-fare bookings. Going moved away from leaning on these for exactly this reason.
  • Misleading-advertising exposure: FTC and EU consumer regulators are aggressive about "save up to X%" claims that aren't substantiated.

Cost & work to start

  • Cost: ~$0 to ~$5k legal review of subscription T&Cs. Stripe billing setup is hours.
  • The product is deal-finding + alert delivery + retention. This is the most natural pivot for the AI engine already designed — the existing price_observations table (see Caching & Indexing) is exactly the substrate for a "deal score".

Important details

  • Auto-renewal disclosure laws: California ARL, FTC Negative Option Rule (2024 update), UK CMA subscription rules. Cancel-anytime + email confirmation of renewal are now the norm.
  • "Up to X% off" claims need substantiation files retained for 3 years in the US, 4 in the EU.
  • App-store fees apply if you sell the subscription through iOS / Android in-app (15% year 1 / 30% on Apple; similar on Google). Selling via the web with magic-link login to mobile avoids this — Spotify-style.
  • This is the cleanest path to real negotiated discounts later: with 50k+ subscribers, airlines and tour operators will talk exclusive offers. The flywheel takes 2–3 years.
Fit Model 3 is the closest match to what's already designed. The engine produces ranked options on total cost, including open-jaw and intermodal alternatives most users wouldn't have searched for. The price-history table compounds into a "deal score" moat over months. No accreditation friction.

Model 4 — Service fee / markup

You add a flat or % service fee on top of a flight/train/attraction sold via a partner. Two variants:

  • Variant A: You're an affiliate; the partner books; you bill the user separately for "planning / concierge" service.
  • Variant B: You're a reseller (Model 2) and add a service fee at checkout. Common at Expedia / Booking ($15–35 / ticket typical), allowed under IATA Resolution 824 and explicitly non-commissionable (you keep 100%).

Upside

  • Predictable unit economics — a flat fee per booking, decoupled from commission rates.
  • Defensible because it's tied to a service (AI planning, multi-stop composition, optimization), not gatekeeping.
  • Travel agents have done this for 50 years; the legal posture is well-trodden.

Downside

  • Variant A is fragile — you can't enforce a planning fee for a booking the user could complete without you. Anti-circumvention clauses are hard to write and harder to enforce against consumers. This works for B2B (corporate travel managers) precisely because of that.
  • Variant B requires you to be a full reseller — back to all of Model 2's overhead.
  • Optical: users compare your total to Google Flights; if you're $25 higher, you lose the click. The fee has to be invisibly bundled, justified by tangible value (status, refund flexibility, AmEx-style concierge), or applied only to change / cancellation flows where it reads as work-for-money.

Important details

  • US DOT Part 399: service fees must be disclosed before payment — not a surprise.
  • EU drip-pricing enforcement (UK Digital Markets, Competition & Consumers Act 2024 and EU equivalents): fees in the total advertised price.
  • Change / cancellation fees ($25–75 typical) are far more defensible than booking fees — users perceive them as work-for-money.

What I'd actually build, in order

Given the AI engine, the price-observations table, and the multi-stop trip composition already designed:

  1. Affiliate (Model 1) is table stakes. Day-one revenue with no legal surface. Trainline / FlixBus / Viator / GetYourGuide / Duffel deep-links earn while the rest of the product matures. The Phase 7 partnerships in the Roadmap are this — bring some of them earlier.
  2. Subscription discount club (Model 3) is the highest-leverage fit. The engine's outputs (cheapest open-jaw, intermodal alternatives, total-cost ranking, price drops over time via price_observations) are exactly what Going sells. No accreditation. The remaining work is curation, alerting, and retention loops on top of search.
  3. Service fee Variant A as a B2B angle. Travel agencies and corporate travel managers will pay for AI planning regardless of who books. White-label or per-seat licensing.
  4. Full OTA (Model 2) only after PMF. A 6–18 month accreditation timeline plus bonded capital is a tax on momentum that's better spent on the engine until the demand shape is known.
Sequence Affiliate now → subscription club at ~6 months → service fee / OTA at scale.

Important details that bite

  • EU Package Travel Directive hits the moment a multi-stop planner sells flight+hotel or flight+train through your checkout, even via affiliate if you take the payment. Stay in pure deep-link mode and the user buys each leg from the operator — you stay out of "package organiser" status.
  • Mistake fares are a magnet for subscription churn drama. Going pivoted away from leaning on them; learn from that.
  • California Seller of Travel is the strictest US state regime — applies to anyone soliciting California residents regardless of where you're based. The $50k bond is the workaround if you don't operate a trust account.
  • UK ATOL is mandatory for any flight package sold to a UK consumer, no de minimis. £2.50 / passenger APC is non-negotiable.
  • Negotiated discounts (IATA Net Rates, consolidator agreements, bulk-purchase) are not realistically available below ~10k bookings / year.
  • Affiliate stacking caution. Some networks (Skyscanner, Kayak) forbid wrapping their links in a paid product. Read the affiliate terms before building a subscription on top.
  • GDPR for trip data. Itineraries are sensitive personal data (location, dates, sometimes companions). A subscription club storing search history needs a DPA, a lawful basis, and a retention policy.

How this connects to the engine

  • Caching & Indexing — the price_observations append-only log is the substrate for "deal score" alerts — the core asset of Model 3.
  • Cost Transparency — total-cost ranking is what makes the curated deals genuinely defensible vs. naive "lowest fare" alerts, and already satisfies DOT Part 399 full-fare disclosure.
  • Ground Transit + Places & POI — multi-stop trip composition is the differentiated capability that justifies the subscription or B2B service fee.
  • Data & Providers — the path from fast-flights (ToS-grey discovery) to Duffel (bookable inventory) is what unlocks Model 2 when it becomes worth it.

Where this slots into the roadmap

Monetization is a parallel track, not a phase. Affiliate integrations can begin alongside Phase 0; subscription rollout naturally lines up after Phase 4 (web UI exists); OTA accreditation only after several months of validated demand. See the Roadmap decision checkpoints for the gating questions.
